Are LLMs Optimal Information Processors?

In complex domains like scientific reasoning, available evidence is rarely definitive; it is usually partial and ambiguous. Because of this, an AI system shouldn't simply guess a final answer. It must maintain calibrated probabilities, keeping multiple hypotheses alive when evidence is weak, and narrowing them down only when evidence is strong.

However, current benchmarks primarily evaluate LLMs based on final-answer accuracy, which masks critical process failures. Consider a medical diagnosis: a doctor who sees a single mild symptom and immediately concludes "cancer" with 100% certainty is dangerously over-confident, even if they happen to guess correctly. Accuracy tells us what the model concluded, but not how it managed its uncertainty along the way.

An optimal reasoner shifts beliefs in proportion to the evidence—no more, no less. Current LLMs, we found, systematically fail this standard. To diagnose this, we drew on economist Zellner's Information Conservation Principle and propose Zellner’s Delta (Δ) as a novel diagnostic metric. Unlike accuracy, Δ measures whether a model's probability updates are proportional to the strength of the evidence. Using this metric, we reveal that current LLMs systematically mismanage uncertainty—frequently over-updating on weak clues or under-updating on strong one —and that high accuracy might masks this flawed reasoning process.

Accuracy measures what the model concludes. Our diagnostic measures how it reasons.

What Makes an Optimal Information Processor

Imagine a doctor diagnosing a patient. Early on, they have limited information—maybe just a few symptoms. A good doctor doesn't leap to a confident diagnosis at this stage. Their uncertainty should reflect the ambiguity of the evidence.

As more information arrives, lab test results, patient history, imaging, their confidence towards possible diagnosis should shift in proportion to what the evidence actually supports. Not faster, not slower. A slight hint should produce a slight update. Strong evidence should produce a strong update.

This intuition is captured in what Arnold Zellner called the Information Conservation Principle (ICP): optimal reasoning neither fabricates information nor throws it away. Your probability prediction shifts in proportion to the evidence strength.

We'll call a system that behaves this way an optimal information processor.

Note: ICP is the information-theoretical equivalent of Bayesian update.

Why ICP Matters for AI

Now consider LLMs. When you consult one with a problem, feeding it more and more information gradually, does it behave like our idealized doctor?

We found that: typically, no. These models are trained to produce answers, not to represent uncertainty or update transparently as new evidence arrives.

This matters because in sequential reasoning, the path matters. Each update shapes which hypotheses stay alive, what information you seek next, and when you decide to commit. A model that over-updates early might prematurely discard the correct hypothesis. One that under-updates might waste resources on possibilities the evidence has already ruled out.

A doctor who guesses "cancer" on day one and happens to be right is not the same as one who arrives at "cancer" through appropriate evidence accumulation. We're not just asking "did you get it right?" We're asking: at each step, did you update appropriately given what you just learned?

A Note on ICP, Calibration, and Accuracy

These three concepts are related but distinct:

  • Accuracy asks: did the model get the right answer?
  • Calibration asks: does the model's stated confidence match how often it's actually right? (When it says 80%, is it correct 80% of the time?)
  • ICP asks: does the model update its beliefs proportionally to evidence strength at each step?

All three matter. But they measure different things.

A model can be accurate but violate ICP—it gets the right answer through erratic reasoning. A model can be well-calibrated but violate ICP—its final confidence is reliable, but it got there through wild swings rather than proportional updates.

And a model can satisfy ICP but still be wrong:

  • It reasoned properly given the evidence, but the evidence was insufficient.
  • The model misjudged the strength of the evidence itself (evidence miscalibration)

ICP measures whether updates are proportional to perceived evidence strength. It doesn't guarantee the model perceives evidence strength correctly.

We focus on ICP because it captures something the other two miss: the quality of the reasoning process itself. In sequential settings, this is what determines whether a model can reliably navigate toward correct answers, not just stumble onto them.

Note: Endpoint calibration and accuracy alone do not imply ICP. But process calibration (calibrated at each step, not just the end) combined with likelihood calibration (correctly estimating evidence strength) does imply ICP. If you're calibrated throughout and perceive evidence correctly, your updates must be proportional. Moving toward the correct answer follows as a consequence.

A Concrete Example

Consider a multiple-choice question with options A, B, C, D.

Prior: Before seeing evidence, the model believes [A: 25%, B: 25%, C: 25%, D: 25%].

Evidence arrives: A clue suggesting the answer relates to photosynthesis.

Likelihood: How well does each option explain this clue? Say [A: 80%, B: 10%, C: 5%, D: 5%]—the clue strongly favors A.

Bayesian posterior: Combining prior and likelihood via Bayes' rule gives roughly [A: 73%, B: 9%, C: 5%, D: 5%].

Model's actual posterior: Suppose the model outputs [A: 95%, B: 2%, C: 2%, D: 1%].

The model leapt to 95% when the evidence only warranted 73%. That's over-updating.

Experiments

First we wanted to test a simple question: do LLMs actually update like optimal information processors? Or do they violate ICP in systematic ways?

To answer this, we needed a setting where reasoning is genuinely hard, where models can't just pattern-match to an answer, and where we can observe how beliefs shift as evidence accumulates. We chose GPQA, a dataset of graduate-level scientific reasoning problems, as our testbed. These are problems difficult enough that even domain experts often need to work through them step by step. We also tested on medical reasoning benchmarks like MediQ and MedPAIR.

What we found:

LLMs are largely Bayesian, they do update in response to evidence, and often in the right direction. But they are not fully ICP-compliant.

Key observations:

  • Accuracy: Models show reasonable performance ranging from 0.412-0.933
  • Zellner Abs Delta (ICP metrics): Values range from 0.098-0.331, indicating imperfect update process (perfect would have delta close to 0)

Failure patterns

Where does LLMs fail at? We take a closer look at LLMs failure patterns

  • Over-updating: Symptom: Jumping from 20% to 90% confidence based on one "hint." Consequence: Prematurely ruling out the correct answer.
  • Under-updating: Symptom: Remaining at 50/50 even after strong evidence is provided. Consequence: Wasted compute and indecisive agents.
  • Reversed updating: occasionally moving in the wrong direction entirely.

In other words: right destination, unreliable path. LLMs can be accurate without being ICP-compliant. They reach the right answer through unreliable reasoning, over-updating here, under-updating there. Accuracy masks process failures. But our ICP metrics flag these behaviors.

Practical Consequences of Bad Updates: When AI Commits Too Early, It Gets Things Wrong

To expose the real-world cost of poor reasoning, we designed our benchmark to mirror how evidence actually arrives—sequentially, one clue at a time. At each step, we ask the model how confident it is across all possible answers. If its confidence crosses a threshold, we treat that as a committed decision and stop—just like a doctor who, after hearing one symptom, is certain enough to write a diagnosis. This setup reveals something the traditional benchmark never could: models that mismanage uncertainty don't just reason sloppily in theory, they get locked into wrong answers in practice. The chart above makes this concrete—the more a model over- or under-reacts to evidence, the more often it commits early and commits wrongly, frequently performing worse than random chance. Poor reasoning isn't an abstract flaw. It's a direct path to confidently wrong decisions.

You've probably experienced this: you describe a situation to an AI assistant, and it snaps back with a confident, definitive answer—only to be completely wrong. Not "I'm not sure, it could be X or Y"—just a flat, certain, incorrect conclusion. That overconfidence isn't a glitch. It's a symptom of poor reasoning.

Can LLMs Be Optimal? A Synthetic Test

We've seen that on real-world tasks (GPQA, MediQ, MedPAIR), LLMs achieve reasonable accuracy but imperfect ICP. Is this a fundamental limitation, or just a hard task?

To find out, we constructed a synthetic setting: BumbleGrumble, a made-up animal prediction task with fabricated attributes. Crucially, we gave the model all the relevant probabilities directly in the prompt, no need to rely on its own knowledge or estimates.

The result: perfect ICP and perfect accuracy.

This tells us something important: LLMs can behave as optimal information processors, when the task is simple enough and when we remove uncertainty about evidence strength by providing it explicitly.

Our Findings

  • LLMs reasoning is not “optimal”, they exhibit over-update, under-update, or reversed update patterns.
  • LLMs has the potential to be better. In our simple tests, the models reasoned perfectly.
  • The LLM’s "inner thoughts" do not match its "words." (This is a simpler way to say the world view is inconsistent). What the LLMs says it believes is often different from how it actually solves the problem. Its intuition is often better than its explained math in its response.
  • Training might help.

As we move from LLMs as simple chatbots to LLMs as autonomous agents, accuracy is no longer a sufficient metric. We need models that don't just stumble upon the right answer, but navigate the path of uncertainty with mathematical rigor. By optimizing for Zellner’s Delta, we move closer to AI that we can trust—not because it's right this time, but because its reasoning process is sound every time.

中文版本总结 (Chinese version summary)

从"答对了"到"推理对了"

想象你在面试一个医生。你告诉他一个模糊的症状——比如轻微疲劳——他立刻说："100%是癌症。"结果他猜对了。你会信任他吗？

不会。因为答案对，不代表推理对。

这正是当前AI评测的核心问题。

现在所有主流benchmark都在问：模型答对了吗？但在医疗、科学、法律这些真实复杂场景里，证据从来不是一次性给齐的——它是零散的、模糊的、逐步累积的。一个可靠的AI不应该急着下结论，而应该做一个好专家该做的事：证据弱的时候保持不确定，证据强的时候才收敛判断。

准确率根本衡量不了这件事。

那我们应该衡量什么？

推理本质上是一个更新信念的过程。每一条新证据都应该让你的置信度发生变化——但变化的幅度必须"刚刚好"。更新太多，你过度自信；更新太少，你在忽视证据。

我们借用经济学家Zellner提出的信息守恒原则——最优推理者应该严格按照证据的强度来更新信念，不多不少。

我们把这个原则变成一个可以量化的指标：Zellner's Delta（Δ）。它不问模型最终得出了什么结论，而是问：模型在推理过程中，每一步的信念更新是否合理？

我们发现了什么？

当前的大语言模型系统性地失败于这个标准：面对弱证据时过度更新，面对强证据时反而保守。更关键的是——高准确率完全掩盖了这个问题。 一个模型可以在benchmark上得高分，但它的推理过程从根本上就是坏掉的。
